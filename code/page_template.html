The half-life of fame

When someone notable dies, many of us look them up. I followed the English Wikipedia articles of {{n_main|int}} people who died between 2016 and 2023, from a year before their death to as much as five years after it. Pick a group below to see how the attention rises and fades.

This study is about attention, not about the people themselves. A Wikipedia article being read more or less says nothing about the worth of a life. But the pattern across thousands of lives says something about how a culture remembers: how quickly a death becomes news, and how quickly the news becomes the past.

Data: English Wikipedia user pageviews (Wikimedia, CC0), Wikidata (CC0) · Map: Natural Earth (public domain), OpenFreeMap Positron (OpenStreetMap, ODbL)

01 · The spikeA month of looking up

In the month someone dies, the typical person's article is read {{prose.k0|f1}} times as much as it was in the year before, as a share of all English Wikipedia reading. Taking the higher of that month and the next (for deaths late in a month), the median peak is {{peak.median|f1}} times the usual level, and a quarter of people see more than {{peak_quart.2|f0}} times. Then it falls away fast. The month after the death the median is {{prose.k1|f2}}, by the second month {{prose.k2|f2}}, and a year on {{prose.k12|f2}}, with a small bump at the first anniversary.

Median across {{n_main|int}} people, with a 95% interval.

02 · Two speedsFast, then slow

The fall has two speeds. A model with two decaying parts fits the curve more closely than a single exponential or a power law: the first rush has a half-life of about {{prose.hl_fast_days|int}} days, and the remaining extra attention fades with a half-life of about {{prose.hl_slow|f1}} months. This loosely echoes the idea, from studies of collective memory, of a short burst of news and conversation followed by a slower settling, though what is measured here is looking up, not memory. A power law, the other common shape for forgetting, predicted later deaths slightly better when fitted on earlier ones, so the two-part model is the better description rather than a proven law.

Three decay models fitted to the median curve.

03 · The settling pointBack to where they were

Two to three years after a death, the typical article gets {{lr.median|f2}} of its pre-death share of attention (95% interval {{lr.lo|f2}} to {{lr.hi|f2}}). Death does not, on average, make someone more looked up in the long run. {{lr_above1_pc|f0}}% of people end above their old level and the rest below. One way to judge this is against people who had not died yet: two years before each death, attention to the same articles was drifting down by about {{prose.placebo_y1_pc|abs}}% a year. A second check, exploratory and added after the plan, looks at how attention changed over the two to three years before each death: it fell by about {{prose.placebo36_pc|abs}}%. Measured against that drift, people end about {{prose.vs_drift_pc|f0}}% above where they would otherwise have been (95% interval {{review.drift36.pc_lo|f0}}% to {{review.drift36.pc_hi|f0}}%), assuming the old drift would have continued.

A placebo test: the same people around a made-up date two years before their death.

04 · Who keeps the attentionYoung deaths, and performers

The clearest difference is age. People who died under 50 had a median peak of {{prose.u50_peak|int}} times their usual attention, and two to three years later still had {{prose.u50_lr|f2}} times it. People who died at 90 or over peaked at {{prose.o90_peak|int}} times and settled at {{prose.o90_lr|f2}}. In a model that also allows for occupation, citizenship, sex, fame before death and the year of death, dying under 50 goes with {{H4.lr.age_u50.b|f0}}% more lasting attention than dying at 65 to 79 (95% interval {{H4.lr.age_u50.lo|f0}}% to {{H4.lr.age_u50.hi|f0}}%), and this holds in all {{robust_n|int}} planned checks. It does not show up at a made-up date before the death, so it is not just that younger people's articles were already rising.

Median attention by age at death.

Among occupations, performers (actors, musicians, singers, directors) are the only group whose typical attention ends above where it started ({{prose.perf_lr|f2}}). Religious figures settle lowest ({{prose.rel_lr|f2}}). In the model every other group keeps less than performers, sport by {{H4.lr.fam_Sport.b|abs}}% and politics and law by {{H4.lr.fam_Politics and law.b|abs}}%. Part of these gaps was there before the deaths, though: at the made-up earlier date, sportspeople's and scientists' articles were already fading faster. Journalists and religious figures get unusually large spikes, scientists small ones.

Median attention by occupation family (one sampled occupation per person).

Women and men are hard to tell apart. The typical woman settles at {{prose.women_lr|f2}} and the typical man at {{prose.men_lr|f2}}; in the model women come out {{H4.lr.women.b|abs}}% lower, but that difference disappears in most of the checks, so I would not read anything into it. Citizens of English-speaking countries have smaller typical spikes on English Wikipedia ({{prose.en_peak|f1}} times against {{prose.oth_peak|f1}}), perhaps because more readers already know them. Comparing people with the same attention before death, they keep more of it later (+{{H4.lr.english.b|f0}}%), but without that comparison the difference disappears and in a median regression it is only {{robust_range.english.0|f0}}%, so this one is not robust either.

06 · New articlesKnown first in death

Some people get a Wikipedia article only when they die. Of the {{posthumous.n_eligible|int}} people I could follow, {{posthumous.created_k0_k1|int}} ({{posthumous.pc|f1}}%) had an article that was first read, under its current title, in the month of their death or the month after. Their attention has no "before" to compare with, so they are left out of the numbers above. Two to three years later, the typical one of these articles was read about {{prose.posthumous_keep_pc|int}}% as often as in its first full month. A further {{posthumous.first_seen_k2plus|int}} articles were first read two or more months after the death; these are articles either renamed after the death (for example to tell two people of the same name apart), whose earlier views sit under the old title, or written months or years later; the data cannot tell which. They are left out too, and more of them are about women and about people from outside the English-speaking world.

07 · How I did thisPageviews and a plan

People. Wikidata lists {{data.n_people|int}} people with an English Wikipedia article and a date of death in 2016 to 2023. I took a fixed 30% sample by a hash of each Wikidata ID ({{data.n_sample|int}} people) and fetched each article's monthly views by people (not identified bots) from Wikimedia, 2015 to 2026. I dropped dates of death recorded as 1 January (often a year given without a day), the earliest deaths in 2016 (too little "before"), and articles that did not yet exist a year before the death.

Measure. Each month's views are taken as a share of all English Wikipedia views that month, because overall reading has fallen over the decade. Everything is then compared with the person's own average from 12 to 2 months before the death.

Models. I wrote the analysis plan before computing any of the after-death numbers. The main model is a regression of the long-run ratio (on a log scale) on occupation family, sex, age at death, citizenship, Wikidata sitelinks, attention before death, and the year and calendar month of death, with uncertainty clustered by month of death. I reran it {{robust_n|int}} ways: median regression, trimming outliers, leaving out each year and US citizens, raw views instead of shares, and other windows and baselines; the placebo date is a separate test.

What this cannot say. Reading an article is not the same as remembering or valuing a person. English Wikipedia is one audience. Wikidata gives one sampled occupation and one citizenship per person, and the cause of death is not in the data, so sudden and expected deaths cannot be separated. Articles renamed after a death drop out of the "before and after" sample.
